# Add a `preload` option to animated camera movements

## Why

This is [maplibre-preload](https://github.com/AbelVM/maplibre-preload) ported into maplibre-gl-js. The
plugin solves the same problem from outside, and does it well enough that I have been using it — but
working around the public API cost it a lot, and most of that cost is now avoidable from inside.

Concretely, what the plugin has to do today:

| | plugin | this PR |
|---|---|---|
| entry point | monkey-patches `panTo` / `zoomTo` / `easeTo` / `flyTo` | an option on the movement |
| tile lifecycle | hijacks the `Tile` class (a community hack) to reach the manager | one method on `TileManager` |
| custom protocols | only via the hijacked path; otherwise falls back to `fetch` | uses the normal source path, so they work |
| the movement | `async: true` by default — it **waits** for the preload before animating | never blocks, not even optionally |
| the path | reconstructs the flight itself | shares the animation's own `CameraMovement` |
| cache | was pushed onto `fetch` and the browser cache by [maplibre#6041](https://github.com/maplibre/maplibre-gl-js/issues/6041) | the render loop's own tile lifecycle |

The last two rows are the ones I care about most. The plugin cannot reuse the camera's path, so it
reconstructs one; that is a second implementation to keep in step with the first, and it is why
`zoomTo` needed a bug fix of its own in the plugin's 1.1.0. And the movement waiting on the preload
means the wait is paid in full before anything is seen.

What it gives up: the plugin has a `progressCallback({loaded, total, failed})`. I have not built an
equivalent, and I have written that up separately rather than pretend otherwise.

## What you get

- **Arrival at a drawn map.** Measured in Chrome over a throttled tile server, a 4-second
  intercontinental flight arrives to a fully drawn map at 600 ms per tile where it arrived to a third
  of one. At 800 ms per tile, 0% before and 67% after.
- **Far less wasted traffic.** A moving camera's network cost is dominated by requests it abandons.
  Over the same flight the render loop started about 100 tile requests and **abandoned 82 of them**,
  because the viewport covers ground and then uncovers it mid-download. With `preload` that drops to
  **zero**, and total requests fall by two to four times.
- **Three lines, no setup.** `preload: true` on any animated movement. `zoomTo`, `panTo`,
  `zoomAround` and the rest inherit it through `easeTo`. No plugin, no wrapper, no import.
- **Tiles arrive ready to draw.** The preload goes through the ordinary source path, so tiles are
  parsed and uploaded by the same machinery that draws the viewport, and land in the out-of-view cache
  where the render loop already looks for them.
- **Works on the real path, on a globe as well as mercator.** Pitch, bearing, the van Wijk arc, custom
  easing and projection are all handled by construction, because the animation and the preload walk the
  same sampled states. A seeded test asserts that over 24 arbitrary movements, and the flight above
  measures it on a globe rather than only asserting it.
- **The foreground is filled first at a pitch.** Where the camera cannot cover every tile before it
  arrives, it arrives with the near ones drawn and the horizon missing, rather than the reverse. The
  harness reports which of the two happened on every run.
- **Politeness about interruption.** A movement that reaches its destination keeps the requests it had
  in flight, since those are the tiles of the ground it just crossed. Anything that interrupts it gives
  them up, so the tiles under the camera now are not queued behind them.
- **Observable without new API.** A preloaded tile announces itself with `dataloading` like any other,
  so `sourcedataloading` shows a preload's requests already.
- **Cheap.** ~0.59 ms once per movement, down from 2.50 ms in the first working version.

## How it works

`Camera` now builds a `CameraMovement` — the path, the easing and the camera handler — and hands the
same object to both the animation and the preload. The preload samples it, works out what each source
covers at each sample, and requests those tiles through a priority cursor bounded by an in-flight
window. That is the whole design; everything below is a decision about it.

**The destination is read first.** A path ends where it is going, so its last sample *is* the
destination. Reading in camera order, which is what I wrote first, spent the request budget on ground
the camera was about to leave and brought not one of the destination's tiles in before arrival on an
intercontinental flight — and because those trip tiles queued ahead of them, the destination landed
20–770 ms *later* than with no preload at all.

**Read ahead by time, not by sample count.** Two samples is worth 1.26 s of warning on a ten-second
flight but about 50 ms on a one-second flight, because a longer movement is divided into more samples.
The lead is one second's worth, floored at two.

**Cap requests in flight, and share the window.** The preload is a guest in the browser's connection
pool alongside the render loop. `MAX_TILES_IN_FLIGHT = 12` across all sources, each source given an
equal share of what is left, a tile landing making room for the next.

That number came out of a synthetic benchmark that stubs out the request path and so cannot see a
socket limit, which is why I re-measured it in the browser, where Chromium holds about six connections
open to one origin. Twelve is roughly twice that, and it is the optimum of the three I tried. At
600 ms per tile over a 4-second flight:

| window | requests | abandoned mid-flight | outstanding at arrival |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 53 | 23 | 6 |
| **12** | **36** | **0** | **6** |
| 24 | 46 | 5 | 18 |

Too small and the camera catches up with tiles that have not landed, which puts the churn back. Too
large and the preload holds requests that queue behind the render loop's, which is the thing the
constant exists to prevent.

**Arriving is not the same as being interrupted.** Keeping the requests on interruption is the obvious
reading of "do not waste bytes already spent", and it costs **14.7% of viewport recovery**: 4533 ms
against a 3951 ms baseline, three runs each, ±15 ms. Cancelling on arrival instead throws away bytes
already spent and re-downloads ground the user has only just seen. With the split, a redirect recovers
in 3943 / 3943 / 3941 ms and arrival quality is untouched.

Telling them apart needs the one thing only the render loop knows, which is that it reached the end of
the path. That matters because the routes are not the same code path: a new movement reaches the
preload through `Camera.cancel`, but a gesture stops the movement from `HandlerManager` and arrives at
precisely the point a natural completion does. Until I separated them, **every gesture interrupting a
flight held its requests and paid the full 580 ms** — the most common interruption was the one case I
had got wrong.

**A drained queue is not a finished preload.** Settled tiles are pruned on every pass and the window
topped back up. Ending the preload whenever nothing was outstanding killed it as soon as the
destination landed, leaving the rest of a long flight unloaded, which is most of it.

I also tried raising `MAX_FRAME_COUNT` from 16 to 48 and rejected it: trip coverage did not improve
(50% against 53%), because more samples shrink a fixed sample lead in time terms and the two effects
cancel.

## Implementation

- **`src/ui/tile_preloader.ts`** — new. Samples the path, works out each source's covering tiles per
  sample, requests them through a priority cursor bounded by the window.
- **`src/ui/camera_movement.ts`** — new. The path extraction shared by the animation and the preload, so
  the two cannot drift apart.
- **`src/ui/fly_to_arc.ts`** — new. The van Wijk helpers, lifted out of `camera.ts`.
- **`TileManager.preloadTiles()`** — a preloaded tile is in neither the in-view set nor the out-of-view
  cache while it loads, so none of the render loop's per-frame work over the tiles it draws is spent on
  a tile the camera has not reached. It joins the out-of-view cache on landing, which is where the
  render loop already looks.
- **`TileManager.abandonPreloadedTiles()`** — gives up on those requests for an interrupted movement.
- **`Camera._easeArrived`** — records that a movement reached the end of its path.
- **`Tile.isSettled()`** — whether a preload's tile has landed, failed or expired.

## Known limitations

**Sub-second movements dip in in-flight coverage.** On a 0.88 s movement with the destination partly
cached, mid-flight coverage falls from 65% to 59%. The destination still arrives about 700 ms earlier
and the movement is over in under a second, so I think the trade is worth it, but it is a real
regression. It is not the window's fault — sweeping 6, 8 and 12 moves it by a few points at most. The
cause is that trip tiles are requested at `t=0` for a camera that arrives before they could land. A
duration-aware window is the fix.

**A tail of requests for ground already behind.** When the network is slower than the flight, the
preload asks ahead and the camera reaches tiles before they land, so up to one window's worth can
still be outstanding for ground behind it at arrival: 6 of 36 requests at 600 ms per tile, 12 of 26 at
800 ms. The sweep above shows neither enlarging nor shrinking the window improves it.

**No preloading before a movement is committed**, so no hover-to-preview. That needs a
`Map.preloadTiles(cameraOptions)` and a shared "resolve this movement's target" step; separate work,
written up separately.

**A pitched camera arrives partly loaded, and the shortfall lands in the right place.** On a flight to
pitch 55 over 600 ms per tile, arrival goes from 14% to 57% and requests fall from 96 to 30. Not the
whole frame, and the reason is arithmetic rather than scheduling: a pitched camera covers 42 tiles at the
destination, and four seconds at 600 ms per tile across six connections affords roughly forty tile
fetches in total. Raising the window from 12 to 24 changes nothing at all — same 57%, same 30 requests —
so this is the flight's duration against the network latency, not the preload's configuration.

What makes 57% acceptable is *which* tiles are missing. Screen y grows downward, so a smaller y is
nearer the horizon:

| | mean screen y, tiles ready | mean screen y, tiles missing |
|---|---|---|
| no preload | 263 | **152** — mid-frame, so the centre of the map is blank |
| with preload | 215 | **50** — the top strip, so the horizon is blank |

The preload inherits `coveringTiles`' distance ordering and spends its budget on the near tiles, which at
a pitch are the magnified ones at the bottom of the frame. The harness now reports this and says which
case it is in, because the percentage on its own cannot tell the two apart.
